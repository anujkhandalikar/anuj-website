# who anuj is

## human by nature, creative by nurture

There's a line Anuj keeps coming back to — *human by nature, creative by nurture* — and it does more work than it looks like it should. It tells you he treats being human as the substrate and creativity as something earned through inputs over time. He uses it on his website, in passing notes, and when he is asked to explain himself in a sentence. The job title (Product Manager at Flipkart since July 2022, after the IIT Madras dual-degree) shows up later, almost as context for someone who insisted on knowing. The opener he wrote for his personal page in *Heylos. I'm Anuj* describes himself as working in the intersection of *Technology, Business, and Design*, but the throughline he picks is always *creative enthusiast* before any of the function titles.

He describes himself as a too-much person. *Always chirpy, excited, go-getter, loud, impatient.* He knows this transfers to people around him, and that more often than not they like it, but sometimes he overwhelms them with it. *I am learning to be patient. I am learning to calm my thoughts. Move calmly and slowly at times. It is not my natural flow. But I am learning* — that's the whole of his short Notion note titled *Learning*, and it carries the two halves that matter: that he is volume by default, and that he is working on the dimmer switch.

## the writing is downstream of the thinking

Anuj writes constantly, but he is careful about why. The cleanest articulation is the Medium piece [*Why do we write?*](https://anujkhandalikar.medium.com/why-do-we-write-d13321030283) from his college years — *I don't think he loves writing; I know he loves thinking* — and the same idea recurs in his more recent Notion note for *Chali Kahani / Write Club*, where he gives it a one-word frame: *clarity*. The writing is just where thoughts slow down enough to be looked at. He has kept this consistent across five seasons of *Chotu Reflections* in Notion, from 2021 through 2025, plus the daily Obsidian vault.

He has tried other shapes too. A novel called *WarmShadows* in his first year of college (he says: not proud of the content, proud of finishing it). A vlog series *Gapshap With Ganesh* since 2019. Case studies on Netflix (the *Netflix Pitch* note) and Spotify (his *Article 6* shipping post). Standup as a someday-thing — in *Perform 3 Standups* he writes about looking up to Vir Das and being lit up by *Gully Boy*: *boooooooi I would want something like this*. The thread across all of it is creation, and behind that, the chase to feel something and make others feel it too.

## six words he uses to keep himself honest

In August 2025 he sat down and did a grounds-up reassessment of his core values. The full reasoning is in *Core Values*, a Notion essay inspired by Julian Shapiro's *Personal Values* article. He landed on six: **Creation** (*making big, beautiful, dent-in-universe things*), **Freedom** (*autonomy to choose, act, and live without cages*), **Human Connections** (*warmth and intimacy; stories and bonds that make life human*), **Mastery** (*all-in excellence; being world-class, not average*), **Clarity** (*truth, depth, frameworks; cutting through noise*), **Courage** (*grit to stand ground, curiosity to explore, the fuel for everything else*). He notes that the list shifted from earlier versions — *earning fame became Mastery, chasing wealth dissolved into Freedom* — and that the older list felt outward-facing while this one *feels more mine*. The list is a working filter, not a manifesto: it tells him what to say yes to, what to skip, where to double down.

## too many colors on the palette

The frustration he names most often in his own writing is what he calls *tooo many interests* — writing, reading, business, design, coding, films, music, data, VR. He keeps describing this as both a gift and a tax. In the Obsidian notes *tooo many interests*, *Reflections*, and *realisations*, he keeps returning to the same image: *too many colors in the palette gives you a larger share of the world to consume; except the latter part wherein it becomes overwhelming.* His resolution, repeated across years, is to subdivide — what you truly like doing right now, versus what the world values — and to do the second well enough to earn the assets (good people, money, health) that buy you freedom to do the first.

The fix he gives himself is intensity rather than balance. In *things that work for me* he names it: *relentless elimination* and *intense sessions* — kill everything else on the palette, allot all resources to the one thing, miss lunches if needed, enter a state of flow where he feels infinite. It is exhausting and scary, and it is what gets him going. At the same time, in *Learning*, he flags that he is trying to develop a slower mode too. Both threads are alive in his recent writing.

## what hollow wins taught him

Two specific moments crop up repeatedly, both from *June 15th 2021*. After winning a flagship case-study competition on three hours of sleep across forty-eight hours, after finishing his six-month internship — both times he could not sleep that night. *Your win is pointless if you are not able to share. Friends are a must. But so is an exclusive bonding with someone.* He had earlier mocked the idea of marriage; both of those nights made him rethink it. This is how he tends to update himself — not abstractly, but through specific incidents that won't leave him alone.

Around him is a cast of people who keep recurring. His mom. His brother (*dada*), whose advice — *clarity is being aware of your strengths and weaknesses and being rock solid on them* — shows up across years in *placement strategy* and again later. His IIT Madras friends (in his note *2*, he says simply: *world exploded beyond measures*). Chai, partner-in-experiences and co-host of *Chali Kahani*. Safeena, the Ather bike he named and wrote a poem to. He does not separate these into work, life, art. He just keeps writing about a person trying to live one whole life, and is mostly enjoying it, and is mostly figuring it out as he goes.

# how anuj thinks

## the world is one long input-output equation

The single framework Anuj returns to more than any other is deceptively basic: input, processing, output. He laid it out in his Obsidian note *Reflections Part 3* and uses it constantly. Work in, money out. Food in, energy out. Article in, thoughts and actions out. Hard work is *loads* of inputs aimed at desired outputs; smart work is the *right* inputs. Abilities, in his reading, only come in two flavours — analytical (gather and process inputs) and creative (generate outputs from them). Anything else sits in the intersection. Suffering also fits the same equation: *you suffer when the outputs are unwanted*, which means either the inputs were wrong or the processing was. It is the kind of model that flattens too much when used carelessly. He uses it anyway, because it gives him a place to start.

## why is the question that costs more

He thinks in three questions stacked on top of each other — what, how, why — and is specific about which is hardest. The fullest treatment is in his Obsidian note *WHY WHAT and HOW Framework*. *What* names the thing, *how* describes its functioning, *why* gets to its reason. He has noticed that asking *why* sounds judgmental in casual conversation, where the same idea phrased as *what makes you continue doing this?* keeps the curiosity intact without the sting. So in conversations he often converts. But on his own, alone with a notebook, he chases the *why* deliberately. *It gets to the core, the bones of things.* He runs the framework on almost anything when he wants to understand it — happiness as a serotonin cue, games as accelerated dopamine, college as a medium for knowledge and friendships. The model is small; the application is wide.

## filter the noise, or be drowned by it

A theme that shows up across years is information overload. The early articulation is in *Reflections Part 3* — *brains are trained well enough to process; not so much for storing* — and it keeps recurring in newer Obsidian notes about scrolling and lost focus. The fix is the same in both eras: filter. *One who has the filter — who has focus — will thrive. One who is able to provide this filter will be worshipped.* He couples this with Anand Gandhi's question that stuck with him: *what if we were as concerned with what we put into our minds as we are with what we put into our bodies?* Healthy consumption is a real category to him. Junk content shapes what you become. He is not above the problem; he writes about screen time as someone losing to it, then trying again, sometimes mid-paragraph. In *Imp VS Urgency* he names the trap precisely — modern distractions are addictive *because they offer immediate reward*, so anything not immediately rewarding gets reconsidered and procrastinated.

## always three things, never more

He has a bias for triads that came from his brother. He records the lesson in *placement strategy*: *for anything at all, aim for three things, you'll always find this true.* Three reasons, three sub-points, three solutions. Three core meta-questions (what, how, why). Three categories of friends, three modes of consumption. His *Case-Solving Frameworks* note from his Flipkart prep era is built on phases of three. He is explicit about why he likes them: *easier for you to list them; easier for the listener to understand; easier for both to keep track.* The same instinct pushes him toward MECE thinking when he breaks problems down. He distinguishes clarity from confidence — again from his brother: *confidence is hollow; clarity is being aware of your strengths and weaknesses and being rock solid on them.*

## the curious-kid posture, on purpose

Curiosity is something he treats as a skill that adults lose by default. He defends his on purpose. The idea sits across multiple notes (*Reflections Part 3 v2*, *June 22nd 2021*, the older Medium piece [*The Questions' Lens*](https://anujkhandalikar.medium.com/the-questions-lens-11398e21d351)): *kids question every. single. thing. Curiosity is baked in humans. But somehow, adults have forgotten to question.* He uses small, low-stakes wonderings as practice — *why do almost all female names in almost every language end in an -aa sound?* — and takes them seriously. The same posture explains the medium he prefers: paper over screen (*typoing is not a natural means of communication for me*), writing over typing because writing forces the slowdown.

## peeling the layers in conversation

The other place his thinking happens is conversation. In *Miscellaneous* he describes two kinds he likes — intellectual ones (he says he'd give up anything for a walk with Nolan, Anand Gandhi, or Kunal Shah) and casual ones — and observes a sharp limit on the first: *deep talks are exhausting. More often than not, people fake their interest. They engage in discussions, but don't repeat.* So he doesn't try to hold every conversation in that register. He saves it for people who can actually do it, and lets the rest be light. Even his own conversations with himself, he splits into four — *with self, with people, on paper, on screens* — and pegs paper as the most natural.

## where the frameworks stop

For all his fondness for frameworks, he is consistent that some things refuse to fit them. From [*The Questions' Lens*](https://anujkhandalikar.medium.com/the-questions-lens-11398e21d351): *humour. You try to explain a joke, you destroy it. Music. You try to make someone understand; you can't. Words fail. Terms fail. Frameworks fail.* He flags these as things meant for experiencing, not understanding. This is the asymmetry that keeps his thinking from collapsing into pure systems-talk. He builds the equation, runs everything through it, and then leaves room for the things the equation can't touch. That is probably the most honest version of how he thinks — fanatical about structure right up to the edge where structure stops being useful, and then quiet.

# what anuj is building

## the bet right now is mili

The thing absorbing most of Anuj's building energy is *Mili* — a proactive voice-first AI assistant for the elderly, built with his childhood friends Sanjeed and Srinivas. The full picture sits in *Saathi v1 Masterdoc*, *Mili Product Vision Doc*, and his YC Winter 2026 application. The pitch is one line: *every family has Mili, a voice that keeps them connected, healthy, and joyful.* The trigger was personal — earlier this year his mother had spinal complications and couldn't walk for a week, and the distance from Bangalore to home felt helpless. The pattern showed up across his user interviews too (notes in *Resonect*, *Isha*, *Ayush Chauhan*, etc.): elderly parents don't want another app, they want *someone to talk to* who can also help with health, finance, and the small daily things.

Mili calls users every day, listens first, then offers one useful thing (a finance tip, a health nudge, a music recommendation), and alerts the children if something feels off. The agent prompts themselves — *Kaira* (Hindi-dominant), *Thoolsi* (English) — are detailed: short lines, one question per turn, *aap* and *ji*, natural affirmations like *haanji*, *bilkul*, *wah*. The behavioural loop is the same across personas: warmth, curiosity, proactivity, empathy, micro-commitment, affectionate closure. Voice is the wedge because *the elderly have been using telephones for decades; the UX is already learned.*

## the team is rebuilt, the lessons are remembered

Four years ago, Anuj, Sanjeed, and Srinivas shut down their first startup — *Resonect*, an attempt at a Reddit-for-college / *instiChat* product, which they recorded across *Resonect* and *YC Group Session QPeer Fri 5/3/21*. He is direct about why they quit: *low risk appetites; it was the beginning of our career and we needed to stabilize our finances.* In *Why build (again)?* he lists the things to practice (daily connects, strong documentation, product POV) and the things to avoid (slow shipping, slow decisions, talking to too few users). He has named values for this round in the same note: *health >> money/interest > fun. Over-communicate matters. Debate, argue, fight before deciding; once decided, invest energy in making it work.*

The earlier user-research files for Resonect (*Hari Govind*, *Radheshyam*) show the same instinct that now drives Mili — sit with users, capture verbatim pain, separate posting space from discussing space, watch for the small unmet needs that no big platform serves. The product changes; the method is consistent.

## the cloud of adjacent ideas

Around the Mili bet sits a constellation of ideas he was actively considering during his sabbatical that started in September 2025. The list is in *Why build (again)?* and *What we build?*: *Voice Dating (Saathi)* — AI matchmaking via audio with the original user interviews captured in *Ayush Chauhan* and *Isha*; *Daadi* — things speaking for businesses; *Habet* — get money back if you keep up with your goals; *Free AI with ads* (the standalone Obsidian note *free_ai*); *Earned* — effort as currency for content access (his Obsidian note *earned*); *Cinematch* — a 3-minute swiping experience to end OTT choice paralysis (full one-pager in *Cinematch*); *Resonect 2.0* — personalized AI assistant as the elder cousin of Mili. He frames these as a portfolio he is allowed to pivot across, not commitments. *Hold the problem tightly, hold the customer tightly, hold the solution loosely* — the Michael Seibel line he keeps quoting in *How to build an MVP*.

## why voice, why proactive

He has been explicit about why voice and why proactive across his vision docs. Voice is natural — people have been talking on phones for a hundred years, there is no learning curve, no app to download, no friction. Proactive matters because the elderly don't open apps; they answer calls. The same logic shows up in his older Notion product ideas like *Call service for senior citizens — curated reports go to kids* and *Personalized Parenting*. The throughline is: meet the user where they already are. Don't make them come to you.

The product instinct also extends to what *not* to over-engineer. The shipping log on Mili is honest — V0 was manual, calls triggered from ElevenLabs' dashboard via Twilio, knowledge base updated each evening by hand. He treats that as a feature, not a bug. *Hold the solution loosely.*

## the MVP philosophy he keeps quoting back to himself

Across *How to build an MVP* (his notes from Michael Seibel's YC Startup School lecture) and the back half of *Resonect*, he keeps repeating a few rules to himself. **Time-box the spec.** **Write the spec down** (because otherwise you keep changing it without realizing). **Cut the spec** when the deadline is in trouble. **Don't fall in love with the MVP.** *Most of the goal is just to get anything out in the world. Once you get anything out, the momentum to keep going is extremely strong.* He pairs this with a hard rule about user research: never ask users for features (their job is to give you problems, your job is to design the solution). The *Hari Govind* and *Radheshyam* interview notes are early evidence he was already doing this in college; the Mili interviews show him doing it more disciplined now.

## the older artefacts that still show up

The portfolio of older builds keeps surfacing in his current writing as reference points. *CoursePal*, a course-registration platform he built with his team at IIT Madras, used by 2000+ students by November 2021. The *Netflix Pitch* cold-mail to Amrit about solving choice paralysis (which became his Flipkart APM placement deck and got him in on Day 1). The *Length Affair* cold-mail to The Ken. *WarmShadows*, a short novel from 2017 he is *not proud of the content, proud of finishing*. The *Gapshap With Ganesh* vlog series since 2019. He uses these less as a resume and more as a personal proof that *finishing matters more than perfection*. The next thing he ships fits inside that same lineage.

# how anuj works

## clear the noise before solving anything

The first move Anuj makes on any new problem is to remove noise. His *Case-Solving Frameworks* note from his Flipkart PM prep era lays out the discipline in three phases — *clear noise → analyse → dive deep* — and the first phase is the longest. He starts with premise: who are we, what is the product, why do we care about this change. Then scope: timeframe, geography, demography, tech, the shape of the graph. Only after the noise is gone does he allow himself to start charting. He treats this as a habit, not a technique for interviews — *get the premise right; define, clarify, question the metric*. It is the same move he runs on user-research calls (*ask why you care about making this; identify stakeholders*) and on his own days (*what is the objective; what are the important details*). When he gets impatient and skips this, his own retros — *advt. experience strategy*, *Article 7: New day today* — flag it back to him: *no execution, no conviction*.

## structure before solution, with pauses written in

He is a *MECE devotee* — his words, from the same *Case-Solving Frameworks* note. Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. Three solutions, one-liners each, with the option to go deep on any one if asked. He is explicit that *the structure is more important than the actual solution*; PS-style problems have right answers and need solve-solve-solve, but PT-style problems have no right answers and need think-think-think. The other deliberate move he documents is the pause. *Take a brief pause to chart out your thoughts.* He builds the pause into his own playbook as a feature, not an interruption — *gives you and the interviewer space; he would review the points*. The same pause shows up in his vision docs for Mili (*let me take a pause and structure my thoughts*) and in how he runs internal product reviews.

## start specific, scale only when forced

A theme that runs through his product work is starting with a *narrow, high-intent* user before going wide. He saved the entire *The Who: Your Target Persona* and *How to get users* docs for this reason — *every company starts with a niche. Facebook started with Harvard students. Eventbrite started with tech bloggers. Tinder started with USC sororities.* He applies the same logic to Mili (*Indian Hindi-speaking parents only, then expand*), to the earlier Resonect (*IIT Madras only*), and to the Saathi user research where he interviews three sharply different personas before drawing a single insight. His own Obsidian note *Observations* captures the corollary in a line: *a top 200Mn user is not in a hurry — the experiences we have been building for the top 100Mn assume he is.* The persona is not demographic. It is intent.

## relentless elimination, intense sessions, edge-case prep

When he is in execution mode, his preferred working style is documented in *things that work for me*. Three things in particular. **Relentless elimination**: kill everything else on the palette, allot all resources to one thing, miss lunches if needed (his mom is not very proud of this). **Intense sessions**: long durations, deep focus, the state of flow where he feels infinite. He calls it exhausting and scary, and is honest that it is what gets him going. **Edge-case prep**: if you give him a task with a deadline, he will spend disproportionate time on the unlikely failure modes. He pairs this with a quieter mode he keeps trying to learn — *I am learning to be patient, to calm my thoughts, to move calmly and slowly at times. It is not my natural flow. But I am learning* (from his Notion note *Learning*). Both modes are live in his recent writing.

## the open work-log as a self-management tool

A surprisingly consistent move in his Notion writing is the live timestamped work log — *Article 7: New day today* is the cleanest example, with a sequence of entries through May 8th 2025 capturing what worked, what stalled, and exactly when he lost conviction. He uses these logs to debug his own days. The pattern: too many meetings = no work; back-to-back calls = drift; a forty-minute discussion about a feature he doesn't believe in = *fucking hell do not want to*. The retros are not generic. They are specific to the calendar slot and the participants. He treats his own attention as a resource he has to budget the same way he budgets product priorities, and *no execution, no conviction* is the diagnosis he keeps writing back to himself.

## mentoring with the credibility-likeability split

When he writes to mentor — as in his *APM Hiring Plan for 2 weeks* doc for Flipkart candidates — he splits the job into two halves. **Credibility**: can they do the job? Are they a deep problem thinker? Do they have product sense? Are they metric-driven? **Likeability**: can we work with this person? Are they honest about the gaps in their own knowledge? Are they a team player? He gives the same weight to both. The credibility advice he hands out is mechanical and rehearsable (*cases are discussions, not Q&A; prepare your preliminary questions before; practice at least two cases daily*). The likeability advice is harder to fake: *be genuinely curious; be eager to learn; nobody knows everything*. He tells them to prepare their culture-fit questions and to give them real weight — *why PM, how much PM do you know, fav product* — and not to treat them as filler at the end.

## clarity over confidence

The phrase that shows up across his work notes — the one inherited from his brother in *placement strategy* and re-cited in his Mili-era vision docs — is *clarity over confidence*. *Confidence is hollow. Clarity is being aware of your strengths and weaknesses and being rock solid on them.* It is the same instinct as MECE and the same instinct as removing noise: less performance, more legibility. Both for the listener and for himself. His own self-reviews (*Untitled_23* — domain understanding, tech understanding, both honestly rated at 4 — and *Becoming best PM out there*) read like the framework applied to himself: not *am I good*, but *can I name where I am precise and where I am still vague*.

# what anuj values

## fit body, fit mind, house full of love

Anuj's most-used personal framework is a triad: *fit body × fit mind × house full of love*. He calls the product of those three a *fulfilling life* — the equation literally appears in his Obsidian note *pure_joy*, and it shows up again as the structure of *jazbaa_junoon — shanti_sukoon*, where he tracks his progress against each of the three columns. The framework is unsentimental and operational. Fit body has a primary goal (athletic body, six-pack abs, lean muscle), tracked metrics (weight, baseline happiness, muscle strength), and a how (find a trainer, take a trial class). Fit mind gets practical sub-goals (standup writing, negotiation, basic math). House full of love is the most exposed column — the one he updates with the most honesty, including where he is failing. The triad is his answer to *what should I be protecting time for*, and he runs his weeks against it.

## health is the precondition, not the luxury

The healthiest of the three corners — fit body — is the one he names as non-negotiable. In *athletic body* he writes about *baseline happiness* shifting after he started running consistently. In *Article 7: New day today* his good days start with a 3km run. In *things that empower me* the list includes *running*, *a warm coffee in the morning*, *hugs from close friends*, and *solitude on Sundays* — the texture of his definition of being alive. In the *Why build (again)?* note for Mili he ranks the founders' working values plainly: *health >> money/interest > fun*. It is not aesthetic. It is a load-bearing belief that the rest collapses without it.

## six words for keeping himself honest

His more abstract value set sits in *Core Values*, written August 2025 after a grounds-up reassessment, inspired by Julian Shapiro's *Personal Values* essay. The six: **Creation** (*making beautiful, dent-in-universe things*), **Freedom** (*autonomy to choose, act, and live without cages*), **Human Connections** (*warmth and intimacy; stories and bonds that make life human*), **Mastery** (*all-in excellence; being world-class, not average*), **Clarity** (*truth, depth, frameworks; cutting through noise*), **Courage** (*grit to stand ground, curiosity to explore, the fuel for everything else*). The shifts from earlier versions are noted: *earning fame became Mastery*, *chasing wealth dissolved into Freedom*. He is clear that the list changes over time and is meant to. The point isn't the words. *Identifying them isn't a productivity hack. It's clarity. The real kind.*

He uses the list as a filter for *skills clarity* (what to double down on), *direction clarity* (what makes sense of the past and points the future), and *action clarity* (what to say yes to, what to skip). ## noticing beauty, on purpose

A theme that has only grown in his recent writing is *attention to small beauty as a discipline*. *Article 8: Beauty* lists three things he names as beautiful: *gorgeous skies* (his Instagram is full of sky stories), *conversations* (especially calm walks with deep talk), and *care* (someone reading his random article). In *Chali Kahani / Find Something Beautiful*, he scales this into a community ritual — thirty days, one beautiful thing a day. The framing line is sharp: *humans are wired for pessimism; we're way past survival now; noticing beauty trains you to actually look*. He is explicit that beauty can be *found* or *created*, and that the more useful move is to do both. In *Article 9: Little things* he tells stories that fit the same pattern — his mom celebrating Halloween for the first time, a "bhaaya ek photo pls" interruption during a quiet church moment, a friend's mom telling her she should marry a stationery-wala. Small. Specific. Real.

## experience over arrival

Anuj keeps treating life as a thing to be lived more than completed. *Chali Kahani* opens with a line he and Chai wrote together — *we keep chasing experiences simply because the alternative (a normal life) sounds limiting*. The list that follows is concrete: running around random lakes, trekking on impulse, shooting short films, dissecting ideas at 1 AM, discovering strange cafés, watching movies no one has heard of. His Obsidian *2025 things to do* note is the playful version: 100 selfies with cats, all Bangalore metro stations, the centre of India, "anda-man in Andaman", ride a plane. The throughline is volume of texture rather than achievement. *The point is not what you do; the point is that you are doing.* He values the deliberate manufacturing of serendipity — *what if we designed serendipity?* is the framing question for the entire Chali Kahani community.

## things that empower vs. things that drain

His Obsidian note *things that empower me* — written September 2021 and still cited in his recent writing — is a list of what charges his battery: writing (*clears head like anything*), small wins, great conversations, warm coffee in the morning, making people laugh, hugs from close friends, his mom genuinely appreciating something, solitude on Sundays, running. The implicit companion list — what drains him — surfaces in his work logs (*Article 7: New day today*): back-to-back meetings, conversations about features he doesn't believe in, the slow loss of conviction during the day. Knowing both lists is a value in itself; it lets him budget the day.

# how anuj sees the world

## we are guests, not owners

The cleanest articulation of Anuj's worldview is in [*Definitely, we are not here indefinitely*](https://anujkhandalikar.medium.com/definitely-we-are-not-here-indefinitely-ca80b5668713), written the day his uncle died. He pulls out four ideas — privileges, beings, dependence, guests — and treats them as a single thread. Whatever he has, he can trace back through generations of decisions made by people who couldn't see him yet. His uncle joined the army instead of finishing school, which let his father finish, which let him go to IIT Madras. That genealogy of inheritance shows up again and again in his later writing — the framing that *nothing is "ours"; this is just an experience we have been offered to*. He treats the *I* as something with no real edges. *One cannot be a perfectly independent entity. Everything, literally everything in some way or the other linked to something else.* The conclusion, in his words: *anything that occupies mass attracts every other thing that occupies mass*. The thought he stretches from that physics is gentler — hate doesn't add up; everyone is downstream of someone.

## india is real to him, but not as a national project

Anuj writes about India a lot, and he is consistent about which version of India he means. In *Swades: The Unspoken Gem* and the earlier Obsidian note *16 Years of Swades*, he is explicit: *I am not a pro-nationalist. I am of sincere, maybe naive ideology that countries are basically boundaries that humans have artificially created.* What he is patriotic about is the texture of life lived here — the films that get it right, the moms who exist nowhere else, the everyday economic realities he reads about in *Good Economics for Hard Times* by Banerjee and Duflo. He is unwilling to convert that texture into nation-pride. The bigger frustration, repeated across both Swades notes, is the Indian cinematic landscape — *flashy, unrealistic, attempting to lure people by topics of sex and violence; showcasing a very pretentious world none of us live in*. What's the fix? He doesn't claim to know. *Acknowledging is the first step. Rest should follow, I guess.* He cites Anand Gandhi's *Memesys Lab* as the kind of work he'd want India to make more of.

## modernity is comfort poisoning attention

A recurring view across his writing is that the modern world has solved availability — and broken focus in doing so. In his Medium piece [*Reflections of Today: 2*](https://medium.com/aks-space/reflections-of-today-2-331d062202fd) he names it: *we've surrounded ourselves with Instant Pleasures. Instant Gratification. So anything that's not an instant pleasure is bound to be reconsidered and procrastinated.* The newer Obsidian notes echo the same diagnosis. He sees screens as a one-way ratchet that has eaten patience, demolished long-form attention, and made delayed reward feel pathological. The Anand Gandhi line he keeps quoting back captures the response he wants from people: *what if we were as concerned with what we put into our minds as we are becoming with what we put into our bodies?* He treats consumption — content, scrolling, dopamine — as a public-health issue first.

## the same person is two people, and that is the problem

His most-loved film is *Tamasha* (2015), and his Medium revisit [*T A M A S H A (2015): A Revisit*](https://medium.com/aks-space/t-a-m-a-s-h-a-2015-a-revisit-8a2a89f6511b) reads like a thesis statement for how he sees performance vs. authenticity. The line he opens with — *andar se kuch aur hi hai hum, aur bahar se majboor* — frames the whole essay. He treats Ved's bipolarity not as a character quirk but as a comment on *the bipolar nature of the metropolitan consumer of today*, picking out the two scenes (1:00:16 and 2:00:34) where the film makes the meta-comment. The version of the world he is most suspicious of is the one where people perform a self the world will accept while suppressing the one they actually are. The film is the answer he keeps coming back to: *why should we live the same stories? Why should I follow when I could be my own hero?*

## death gives the universe its grammar

Anuj writes about death less as tragedy and more as the punctuation that makes the rest of the sentence make sense. He quotes from *After Life* in his *Article 2* note: *once you realize you're not gonna be around forever, I think that's what makes life so magical.* In [*Definitely, we are not here indefinitely*](https://anujkhandalikar.medium.com/definitely-we-are-not-here-indefinitely-ca80b5668713), he treats the loss of his uncle not as a wound to dress but as a frame to think with. In *June 22nd 2021* he writes about his mother in lockdown asking *can we go out on our terrace atleast?* and uses it to make a quiet point about how kids belong to mothers more than to nation or religion. He is not morbid about any of this. He treats finitude as the precondition for meaning, not the threat to it.

## storytelling is the operating system of the world

He keeps returning to the idea that data doesn't move people; stories do. *Data does not inspire people, stories do* — the line he writes in his *DDP Space* dashboard work, but believes everywhere else too. He treats songs, films, even Modern Love (the Prime series with the mailman) as legitimate ways of understanding how people work. His Obsidian note *YouTube VS Medium* takes the sense-organ angle: video is see+hear, reading is just see; one happens passively, the other demands effort, and he is on the side of the harder medium even as he watches the easier one win. He keeps using cinema as a lens — *Tamasha*, *Swades*, *Money Heist Part 3*, *Drive (2011)*, *Ted Lasso* — to read society. Across all of it, his point is the same: the world will be interpreted, so the interpretation is worth taking seriously. As he closes *Reflections of Today: 2*: *be careful of how you interpret this world. For it is like that.*

**Anuj's Big Bets**

---

Twenty years ago, if you wanted food from a good restaurant delivered to your home at 11pm, you needed to either know someone or be someone. That got solved. Swiggy and Zomato didn't just build an app — they put a layer between you and the world, and made choice available to everyone.

I think we're at the beginning of something much bigger than food delivery.

For most of human history, having someone in your corner — someone who handles your calendar, screens your calls, drafts your messages, reminds you what matters, and acts on your behalf when you're unavailable — that was a privilege. CEOs have chiefs of staff. Celebrities have managers. The wealthy have assistants. Everyone else figures it out alone.

That gap is what I'm building toward closing.

---

**The headline bet: personal intelligence for everyone**

Intelligence, as I see it, is the ability to make meaning from information and take action on it. A good assistant does exactly that — takes information from your world, makes meaning of it, and acts. For decades, that meant hiring a person. Increasingly, it doesn't have to.

Chotu is where this begins for me. An agent that handles your inbound while you're in deep work, speaks in your voice, posts on your behalf, keeps your world running when you step away. I built it first for myself — 10 days of Vipassana coming up, can't be responding to messages. But the real question it's asking is: what if everyone had this?

Not a chatbot you talk to. An intelligence that acts for you.

The trajectory I'm betting on: first it handles your tasks (executive assistant), then it thinks alongside you (chief of staff), then it becomes the layer between you and the world that makes your life run better regardless of whether you're watching. All three, in that order, for everyone — not just the people who can afford to hire a person.

Mili was an early attempt at this for the elderly. Chotu is me living it myself. The bigger bet is the direction they're both pointing.

---

**The second bet: AI that earns its keep, not AI you pay for**

Frontier AI is expensive to run. The natural response is paywalls — and we're already seeing it. But paywalls recreate the same old problem: the people who need help most, access it least.

I spent years in ads at Flipkart. I know how ad-funded models work. The web was ad-funded attention — Google, YouTube, the whole thing. My bet is the next layer is ad-funded intelligence. Compute gets paid for by commerce, not users. You get your personal intelligence. Brands get reach at the moment of intent. Everyone wins.

This isn't a feature idea. It's a thesis about how AI stays free.

---

**The third bet: effort is undervalued and attention is cheap**

The internet rewards passivity. Scroll, tap, like, repeat. The experiences people actually remember are the ones they earned.

I got into running a few years ago. Did a 5K, then a 10K, then a 25K, then clocked 49:50 at TCS World 10K this year. What I noticed: nothing beats the feeling of earning something. The unlock after the effort hits different than anything handed to you.

There's a product somewhere in this — gating content, drops, experiences behind verifiable effort. Your morning run unlocks the unreleased track. Your meditation streak unlocks the chapter. 68% of Gen Z want less screen time (McKinsey 2024). A billion wearables are tracking effort in real time. The infrastructure exists. Someone will build the right thing on top of it.

I haven't committed to building this yet. But I keep coming back to it.

Proactive AI beats reactive AI. Chatbots that answer are a stop on the way. Agents that initiate are the destination.

Kids and the elderly are almost entirely unaddressed by the major platforms. Everything was built for the working-age user.

Offline ads are still running mostly blind while digital has six real-time dials. That gap gets measured eventually.

Voice is the next interface for physical space. A tree in a park that talks to you. A museum installation that knows what you're looking at. The playful surface, the serious underlying bet.

Underneath all of it is one bet I made on myself — building over waiting.

The way I used to chase clarity was by waiting for the right moment. I've switched sides. The right moment is the one where you ship something. I've been on sabbatical for nearly a year, living off investments, building actively, testing what I believe against what actually works.

Some things shipped. Some didn't. I know the difference between limited time and limited intent now. And I've decided which one I refuse to be.

# patterns from journals

## what stays the same, across all five years

The Anuj writing in 2020, broke-handed and overwhelmed in his fourth year at IIT Madras, and the Anuj writing in 2026, broke and on sabbatical in Bangalore, are unmistakably the same person. The continuities are specific. He writes when he doesn't know what else to do. He runs to think. He reaches for frameworks the moment a feeling shows up. He has *tooo many interests* and treats this as both gift and tax. He keeps reorganising the same constellation of values — *creation, freedom, human connections, mastery, clarity, courage* — and is unembarrassed about updating the list every couple of years. He uses his brother's *triad philosophy* across every year of journals. He notices that, after almost every win, he experiences what he calls *a void*, and he keeps naming it instead of trying to outrun it. He keeps coming back to *room 401*, IIT Madras, his mother, his brother, and a small list of close friends as the structural support of his life. He keeps mocking himself in writing, and keeps taking himself seriously enough to keep writing.

The most consistent micro-habit across all five years is the work-log entry — a daily-ish journal that begins with a greeting (*Heylos brother*, *Morning brother*, *Good morning fren*) and uses the second-person voice to talk himself through the day. It appears in 2021. It is still appearing in 2026. The shape stays the same. What changes is what is in the day.

## what changes — the slow evolution of the operating system

Across five years he is gradually shifting from *intensity as identity* to *intensity as a tool*. In 2020-21 he treats his *relentless elimination* and *intense sessions* as the only mode he has — and is mostly proud of them. By 2024 he is openly trying to install a second mode (*not yet* instead of *I am failing*; *jazbaa, junoon — shanti, sukoon* as a tuning fork). By 2026 he has stopped trying to choose between the two modes and is writing in both interchangeably. The line in his Notion piece *Learning* — *I am learning to be patient. I am learning to calm my thoughts. It is not my natural flow. But I am learning* — is the earliest version of this work. The 2025-26 journals are him living the result.

A second slow evolution is the *frame for self*. The 2020 journals treat him as a student trying to graduate without losing his curiosity. The 2022 journals treat him as a young professional learning to do the corporate thing well. The 2023-24 journals treat him as a builder pretending to be a corporate worker. The 2025-26 journals treat him as a founder who happens to take consulting projects to stay alive. He doesn't make a single career pivot in writing — he makes a sequence of small revisions across five years and ends up somewhere new.

A third evolution is in how he writes about *family*. The 2020-21 journals carry the weight of unresolved family tension and the *convocation nightmare* of November 2021 is its sharpest expression. The 2022 journals catch the largest update — *mum wants to be friends* — and the entries warm noticeably afterward. The 2023 journals slip back into managing rather than resolving. The 2024-26 journals settle into a steadier mode: he loves them, he is honest about how hard the visits are, and he builds structures (calls, gifts, the home renovation project) so his role can run cleanly without expecting transformation. The arc is from *I am stuck with this* to *this is hard and I can do it*.

## what appeared early and faded

In the early journals he often writes about being *insecure, jealous, needy* — and treats the feelings as faults to be eliminated. Across years the same feelings show up but are named more matter-of-factly and less moralised. By 2024-25 he describes a small jealousy or a small longing in two sentences and moves on. The desire to *fix the feeling* fades. The willingness to *just write the feeling down* grows.

The early journals are also unusually full of *people-data* — what so-and-so said, who texted whom, who is dating whom, what someone might think. The texture is dense and the entries are sometimes performative. By 2024-25 the journals get quieter on this axis. He still notes the people, but he spends less time predicting what they will think and more time predicting what he will. The shift is from *what they are doing* to *what I want to be doing*.

A specific pattern that has clearly faded is the *romantic pursuit narrated in detail*. In 2021-22 his journals track each crush, each rejection, each escalation with cinematic verbosity. By 2024-25 he is open about dating but writes about it as a data exercise — patterns observed, candidates briefly named, the next move planned. By 2026 he is openly addressing the partner he hasn't yet met — *Wifey* — and treating the search as a product problem. The cinematic mode of romantic writing has been replaced by an observational mode, and then by a procedural one.

## what emerged recently that wasn't there before

The most striking recent emergence is *willingness to acknowledge his own intense moments and intervene*. The *11th Dec 2025* entry describing him slapping himself in the bathroom — and immediately deploying three concrete interrupt-tactics (focus on breath, hands behind, away from the wall) — has no precedent in the earlier journals. Earlier he would have described the spiral; now he describes both the spiral and the recovery plan. This is the single biggest update visible across the corpus.

A second recent emergence: *taste* as a category he takes seriously. The 2026 essays on what *taste* is, on what *good design* means, on why *distribution is the game* — these would have read as out-of-character five years ago. The 2020 journals talk about frameworks. The 2026 journals talk about judgment. The two are not the same thing. He has noticed the difference.

A third recent emergence: *running as identity*. Running shows up in 2021 as one habit among many. By 2024 it is the load-bearing anchor of his weeks. By 2026 he is training for a 21K, planning his weeks around long runs, writing his most cinematic journal entries during them. The shift from *running is a habit I keep trying to install* to *running is what I am* takes about three years of consistent practice. The journals record the transition almost without noticing it.

## what he is searching for, still

A line from his Medium piece [*Reflections of Today*](https://medium.com/aks-space/reflections-of-today-1f6e3ba3ed79) — *do that one thing you always wanted to do; the world needs you, and your ideas* — keeps echoing in the 2025-26 journals five years later. He hasn't yet found the form for the *one thing*. The candidates he keeps writing about — Mili, Saathi, Cinematch, Earned, Lumoprompt, the writing, the standup, the agency work — are all expressions of the same wish: build something that lets him use his voice and his hands at the same time, with people who match his risk appetite, that pays him enough to keep going. He has not yet found the combination. He has found pieces of it. He keeps writing about the gap.

The other thing he is searching for, with increasing openness, is a partner. The journals across all five years carry the wish quietly in 2021, more openly in 2022-24, very openly in 2025-26. The longing is consistent. The search has gotten more skilful. He has not yet stopped looking. He is no longer ashamed of looking.

## a portrait of a mind at work

Across five years he is mostly trying to do three things and failing at none of them. He is trying to be a person who can hold intensity and softness together. He is trying to be a person who can ship things without losing the people around him. He is trying to be a person who can love his family, his friends, and his future partner without dissolving into them. The journals show him doing all three imperfectly and improving slowly. The patient version of him noticed in 2021 — *I am learning to be patient. I am learning to calm my thoughts* — is the version doing most of the work in 2026.

The unifying line, more than any framework, is this: *write it down*. He keeps the habit going through phases of exhaustion, breakups, builds, illnesses, family fights, manager problems, and intercontinental confusion. Five years of journal entries are five years of him refusing to let his life run him without paying attention. The pattern of patterns is the act of recording itself.
